# The Great Dispersal — 12 Scripts

Series 1 of the Hot Pepper Trading Company channel. Format: 60–90 seconds, vertical, no intro card, cold open on the contradiction.

Every script uses the same structure:
* HOOK (0–3s) — the contradiction, stated flatly
* TURN (3–15s) — establish what the viewer assumed
* BODY (15–50s) — the actual history
* LAND (50–60s) — the reframe
* SIGN-OFF — "So it is entered in the record."

Narration is written for the ear. Short sentences. Read them aloud before recording; anything you stumble on, rewrite.

Word counts assume roughly 150 words per minute of narration, which is deliberately slower than typical short-form. The pace is part of the brand.

## 01 — Kimchi Was Not Always Red

HOOK
Kimchi was white for most of its history.

TURN
The dish is roughly two thousand years old. The color is about three hundred.

BODY
Cabbage, radish, salt, brine. That was kimchi. If it had heat, the heat came from black pepper, ginger, or garlic — because the chili pepper did not exist anywhere in Asia.

It exists in the Americas. Only in the Americas. Until 1492.

Chilies reach the Korean peninsula in the late sixteenth century, most likely carried by Portuguese traders through Japan. The first Korean text to mention them appears in 1614 — an encyclopedia of collected essays, describing a foreign plant.

And Korea does not immediately embrace it. Early records treat the pepper with suspicion. It takes roughly a century and a half before chili appears in kimchi in the written record, in the seventeen hundreds.

LAND
The most Korean thing in the world arrived by ship, from Mexico, by way of Portugal and Japan. And Korea took a hundred and fifty years to decide it liked it.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Open on white cabbage kimchi. Hard cut to red.
* Aged map: Lisbon to Nagasaki to Busan, drawn in sequence
* Page of the 1614 encyclopedia, or a period Joseon document as stand-in
* Onggi fermentation jars

SOURCE NOTES
* First Korean textual mention: Jibong Yuseol (Collected Essays of Jibong), 1614
* Red kimchi in the documentary record: eighteenth century
* Note: a minority Korean scholarly position argues gochu is native to the peninsula. It is not the mainstream view. Do not engage it in the video; if it comes up in comments, acknowledge the debate exists and cite the 1614 first-mention date rather than arguing.

## 02 — Sichuan's Fire Is Four Hundred Years Old

HOOK
Sichuan peppercorn is native to China. The chili is not.

TURN
Mala — numbing and hot — is two sensations from two continents, and they only met recently.

BODY
The numbing half is ancient. Sichuan pepper has been in Chinese cooking for thousands of years.

The hot half arrives in 1591, in a gardening book by a playwright named Gao Lian, living near what is now Shanghai. He describes a foreign plant. White flowers. Red fruit shaped like the blunt end of a writing brush.

He grows it as decoration.

The chili spends its first Chinese century as an ornamental. Court cuisine wants refinement, not fire. So the pepper goes inland, to the poor, wet, mountainous provinces where salt is taxed and expensive and a plant that makes food taste like something is worth having.

Local records show it reaching Hunan by 1684. Sichuan by 1749.

LAND
The most famous fire in Chinese cooking is younger than the United States is old.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Split screen: Sichuan peppercorn / dried chili
* 1591 Ming-era botanical illustration style
* Map: coastal China, arrows moving inland with dates appearing
* Mapo tofu, shot slow

SOURCE NOTES
* Gao Lian, Zunsheng Bajian, 1591 — first Chinese written record, ornamental context
* First record of culinary use: Shiwu bencao, 1621 edition
* Gazetteer dates: Hunan 1684, Sichuan 1749
* Primary authority: Brian Dott, The Chile Pepper in China: A Cultural Biography
* Arrival route is genuinely contested — say "most likely by sea," not "definitely"

## 03 — Hungary's National Spice Was Turkish Contraband

HOOK
Hungarians did not eat paprika for two hundred years. They hung it on the wall.

TURN
The most Hungarian ingredient on earth arrived as enemy decoration.

BODY
The Ottomans take Buda in 1541 and hold parts of Hungary until 1699. Somewhere in that occupation, the pepper arrives — brought east to west, through Turkish trade.

Hungarians call it törökbors. Turkish pepper.

The nobility grow it in castle gardens because it is pretty, and do not eat it, because they suspect it is poison. Peasants eat it, because black pepper is expensive and this is not.

The word paprika is not coined until 1724. It appears in a Hungarian cookbook only at the end of that century. And it becomes a national symbol in the eighteen hundreds — helped along by cholera epidemics, by Napoleonic blockades that cut off black pepper, and by a machine that could strip the seeds out and make the powder sweet.

LAND
Goulash is a nineteenth century dish flavored with a sixteenth century occupation, using a fruit from Mexico.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Strings of drying paprika on a stone wall
* Ottoman-era map of Hungary, Buda marked 1541
* Szeged and Kalocsa located on the map
* Paprika grinding, close and slow

SOURCE NOTES
* Ottoman occupation of Buda: 1541–1686; Treaty of Karlowitz 1699
* "törökbors" — Turkish pepper — the older Hungarian name
* Word "paprika" coined in Hungarian, 1724
* Sweet paprika enabled by late-1800s seed-removal machinery
* Say "most likely via Ottoman trade" — a minority view credits Portuguese arrival directly

## 04 — Thai Food Without Chilies

HOOK
For most of Thai history, Thai food was not spicy.

TURN
Or rather — it was spicy, but from something else entirely.

BODY
Before the sixteenth century, heat in Siamese cooking came from peppercorns, ginger, and galangal. Sharp, warm, aromatic. Not burning.

The chili arrives with the Portuguese, who reach the kingdom of Ayutthaya in the early fifteen hundreds and establish a trading community there.

What happens next is fast. Unlike China, where the pepper sat ornamental for a century, and unlike Korea, where it took generations — Southeast Asia adopts it almost immediately. The climate suits it. The existing flavor architecture of sour, salty, sweet had a gap exactly the shape of heat.

Within a few generations the chili is not an import. It is the foundation.

LAND
Green curry is a Portuguese trade good in a Siamese kitchen, and it took less than a lifetime.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Peppercorns, galangal, ginger — then chilies dropped in
* Map: Lisbon to Goa to Malacca to Ayutthaya
* Period engraving of Ayutthaya, one of the largest cities in the world at the time
* Mortar and pestle, curry paste

SOURCE NOTES
* Portuguese reach Ayutthaya circa 1511–1516
* Pre-chili heat sources: Piper nigrum, galangal, ginger
* Be careful not to state a precise adoption date; the record is thin. "Within a few generations" is defensible.

## 05 — Vindaloo Is Portuguese

HOOK
Vindaloo is not a Hindi word.

TURN
It is a Portuguese one, mangled.

BODY
Carne de vinha d'alhos. Meat in wine and garlic. A preservation dish that sailors carried on ships because wine vinegar and garlic keep meat edible in heat.

The Portuguese take Goa in 1510. The dish comes with them. In Goa, palm vinegar replaces wine, and something new gets added — a fruit the Portuguese themselves brought from the Americas, that grows extremely well in the Indian climate.

Chilies reach India by around 1500, likely through Portuguese contact on the Malabar coast. Within a century India is not just growing them, it is exporting them. Today India is the largest producer and exporter of dried chili in the world.

The plant is four hundred years old there. The cuisine acts like it is four thousand.

LAND
An Indian restaurant's hottest dish is a Portuguese sailor's preserved pork, seasoned with a Mexican fruit.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Portuguese carrack under sail
* Map: Lisbon → Cape of Good Hope → Calicut / Goa
* Guntur chili fields, aerial
* Vindaloo, shot as a still life

SOURCE NOTES
* Portuguese conquest of Goa: 1510
* Cabral reaches Calicut: 1500
* Etymology: carne de vinha d'alhos → vindalho → vindaloo
* India as largest global producer/exporter of dried chili — verify current figures before publishing

## 06 — West Africa Said Yes Immediately

HOOK
Europe carried the chili everywhere and barely ate it. West Africa adopted it within a generation.

TURN
The people who moved the pepper were not the people who wanted it.

BODY
Portuguese ships round the African coast through the fifteenth century, and after 1492 those same routes carry American plants east. Chilies land on the Guinea coast early — early enough that by the time Europeans are writing detailed accounts of West African cooking, the pepper is already in it.

Part of the reason is that West Africa already had a pepper culture. Grains of paradise. Ashanti pepper. Melegueta. There was an existing appetite for pungency and an existing trade in it.

The American chili did not create a taste. It arrived into one, grew better and cheaper than what was there, and took over.

LAND
Adoption is never about novelty. It is about a gap that was already open.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Grains of paradise alongside dried chilies
* Map: Guinea coast, Portuguese forts marked
* Market scene, dried peppers in quantity
* Jollof, suya, shito — shot plainly, no styling

SOURCE NOTES
* Melegueta pepper (Aframomum melegueta) was a major pre-Columbian West African trade good — "grains of paradise" in European sources
* Keep this episode focused on the pre-existing pepper culture. The Atlantic slave trade is the context here and it is enormous; this episode is not the place to compress it. See Episode 12 note.

## 07 — Calabria Has No Native Chili

HOOK
Italy has no native chili pepper. Calabria would like a word.

TURN
Nduja, peperoncino, arrabbiata — none of it existed before the 1500s.

BODY
Spain brings the pepper to Europe first, within years of Columbus. It spreads through Spanish territory, and southern Italy in that period is Spanish territory — Naples and Sicily under Spanish crown rule.

Northern Europe grows it in monastery gardens and botanical collections and treats it as a curiosity. Southern Italy grows it and eats it, because it is poor, because the climate is right, and because black pepper costs money and this grows in the yard.

That is the pattern everywhere, in every country in this series. The chili is adopted by whoever cannot afford the alternative. Italy's peperoncino is peasant food that outlived the aristocracy that ignored it.

LAND
Every cuisine that loves chili learned to love it while it was cheap.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Calabrian chilies strung and drying
* Map of Spanish Habsburg territory in Italy
* Monastery herbal illustration
* Nduja on bread

SOURCE NOTES
* Peppers reach Spain in the 1490s; documented in Spanish monastery gardens early 1500s
* Naples under Spanish rule from 1503
* The economic argument — chili as the poor person's pepper — is the through-line of the whole series. State it here explicitly.

## 08 — Europe Carried It And Would Not Eat It

HOOK
The people who spread the chili across four continents mostly refused to eat it.

TURN
Portugal and Spain moved this plant to Africa, India, Southeast Asia, China, and Japan. Northern Europe grew it in pots and looked at it.

BODY
The pepper arrives in Europe immediately — Columbus brings it back on the first voyage. It is in European botanical texts by the 1540s.

And then, largely, nothing. In England, Germany, France, the Low Countries, it is a botanical specimen. A curiosity. Occasionally a medicine. Not food.

Why? Because northern Europe already had access to black pepper, and black pepper was expensive, and expensive is what made it desirable. The chili's problem in wealthy Europe was that anyone could grow it. It had no status.

Meanwhile in Sichuan, in Goa, in Guinea, in Calabria, that exact quality — anyone can grow it — is why it won.

LAND
The chili conquered the world by being worthless.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Sixteenth century European herbal illustration of Capsicum
* Northern European still life, black pepper in a silver vessel
* Split: European greenhouse pot / Indian field to horizon

SOURCE NOTES
* Capsicum documented in European herbals by the 1540s (Fuchs, Dodoens era)
* This is the thesis episode of the series. If one video is going to travel, this is the candidate.

## 09 — The Pepper Was Never Meant For Us

HOOK
Chilies did not evolve heat to hurt you. They evolved it to hurt mammals in general.

TURN
And it worked, until one mammal decided it liked it.

BODY
A pepper wants its seeds carried and dropped somewhere useful. Birds do that well — they swallow whole, they fly far, and their digestion leaves the seed intact.

Mammals do it badly. Molars destroy seeds.

So the plant produced capsaicin, which binds to a heat receptor mammals have and birds effectively do not. To a bird, a ghost pepper is just fruit. To a mammal, it is a warning.

The system worked for millions of years. Then, somewhere in Mesoamerica, humans ate the thing designed to repel them, and decided the pain was interesting.

LAND
Every hot sauce in your kitchen is a defense mechanism that failed spectacularly.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Bird taking a wild pequin, slow
* Simple diagram: TRPV1 receptor, bird vs mammal
* Wild chiltepin on a desert shrub
* Modern hot sauce shelf, deadpan

SOURCE NOTES
* Directed deterrence hypothesis — Tewksbury and Nabhan
* Birds lack the capsaicin sensitivity in TRPV1 that mammals have
* Avoid saying birds "can't taste" it — the mechanism is receptor binding, not taste

## 10 — The Hundred Year Map

HOOK
It took the chili pepper roughly one hundred years to reach every major port on earth.

TURN
No cultivated plant has ever moved that fast.

BODY
1492, the Caribbean. 1493, Spain. By 1500, the Portuguese are carrying it around Africa. Goa by 1510. The Guinea coast in the same window. Ottoman territory by mid-century. China by 1591 at the latest. Japan, Korea, the Philippines, Southeast Asia, all inside the same span.

A hundred years. From one hemisphere to every inhabited continent.

Compare that to anything. Maize took longer. The tomato, which traveled the same ships from the same place, took two hundred years just to be accepted as food in Europe.

The chili moved fast because it was small, dried well, kept forever, grew anywhere warm, and reproduced from its own seed. It was, functionally, the perfect cargo.

LAND
It did not need anyone to plan its distribution. It only needed ships.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS

* End on the completed map, held for three full seconds

## 11 — The One Place It Did Not Take

HOOK
The chili conquered every inhabited continent. It never conquered the kitchen it arrived in first.

TURN
Northern Europe. And to a degree, Japan.

BODY
Japan received chilies in the sixteenth century, same window as Korea, likely through the same Portuguese contact. Korea rebuilt its entire cuisine around them.

Japan did not. Togarashi exists, shichimi exists, and both are real — but as accent, not architecture. Japanese cooking kept its foundation in dashi, soy, miso, and restraint.

Two neighboring countries. Same arrival date. Same trade route. Opposite outcomes.

The lesson runs through this whole series: a plant does not decide whether a cuisine adopts it. Economics decides. Climate decides. And most of all, whatever the cuisine was already trying to do decides.

LAND
The chili never persuaded anyone. It only ever accepted invitations.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Shichimi togarashi in a small vessel — restraint
* Hard cut to Korean gochugaru by the kilogram — volume
* Map: single arrival point, two divergent lines
* Dashi being strained

SOURCE NOTES
* Both Japan and Korea receive chilies in the late 1500s via Portuguese-Japanese contact
* Do not frame this as one cuisine being better. Frame it as divergent outcomes from identical inputs.

## 12 — Everything You Think Is Ancient

HOOK
Pick the spiciest dish you can think of. It is younger than the printing press.

TURN
A closing inventory.

BODY
Kimchi's red — three hundred years.
Sichuan mala — four hundred.
Hungarian goulash as we know it — two hundred.
Thai curry's heat — four hundred and change.
Vindaloo — five hundred.
Peperoncino — five hundred.
Piri piri — five hundred.

Every one of them is a post-1492 invention wearing the costume of an ancient tradition. And every one of them is now inseparable from a national identity that predates it by millennia.

That is not a debunking. That is the most interesting thing about food. Traditions are not old. Traditions are what a culture does with what arrives.

LAND
The chili did not change what these places were. It gave them a new way to be exactly what they already were.

SIGN-OFF
So it is entered in the record.

VISUALS
* Rapid cut sequence, one dish per line, each with a date stamp
* Return to the completed origins map
* Compass rose, hold

## Production Notes

Publish order: 09 first, not 01. The bird-and-capsaicin episode is the least culturally sensitive, the most universally surprising, and the best test of your format before you touch any national cuisine. Then 08 (the thesis), then 01, then the rest in any order, with 10 and 12 held for last.

The recurring economic argument — chili wins wherever black pepper is unaffordable — appears in episodes 03, 07, 08, and 12. That repetition is deliberate. It is the series' actual thesis and it should feel like a drumbeat, not a coincidence.

Comment policy: for every episode touching a national cuisine, expect people who will experience "your national dish is a recent import" as an insult. The framing in episode 12 is your defense and you should deploy it early rather than defensively — traditions are not diminished by being young. Have that sentence ready as a pinned comment.

Never claim a first date with more precision than the record supports. "First written mention" is a defensible phrase. "Arrived in" is not, unless you have a documented shipment.
