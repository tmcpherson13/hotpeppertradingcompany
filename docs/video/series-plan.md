# Hot Pepper Trading Company — Video Series & Brand Build-Out Plan

Prepared August 2026. Tool prices and model names are current as of this writing and move fast — re-verify before subscribing.

---

## Part 1 — The Channel Architecture

One channel. Four series. Do not spin up separate channels; the audience overlap is total and you need the compounding.

Channel name: Hot Pepper Trading Company
Channel tagline: Selection with intent. Trade with purpose.
Channel promise, stated in the About and implied by every video: this is the trading house that keeps the record of how fire crossed the world.

The four series map to a deliberate funnel:

| Series | Function | Episode count | Hook type |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Great Dispersal | Reach — viral entry point | 8–12 | Surprise / contradiction |
| Trade Routes | Retention — serialized arc | 18 | Serial continuity |
| One Pepper, One Port | Volume — evergreen library | 190 potential | Search / identification |
| Consortium Journeys | Conversion — brand and product | 4–6 per blend | Atmosphere / world-building |

Run them in that order but with overlap: start Great Dispersal alone, add Trade Routes at week 5, add One Pepper One Port at week 9 as filler between arc episodes, hold Consortium Journeys until you have a product to attach it to.

## Part 2 — Series Specifications

### Series 1 — The Great Dispersal

Premise: peppers are American. Every cuisine famous for them is not. Why?

This is your highest-ceiling series because each episode is a genuine "wait, what?" for most viewers. Korean gochujang, Sichuan mala, Hungarian paprika, Thai curry, Indian vindaloo — none of these existed before 1492. That is a legitimately startling fact and it travels.

Format: 60–90 seconds. Vertical. Cold open on the contradiction, no intro card.

Episode slate:

1. Kimchi was white for a thousand years — how Korea got the red
2. Sichuan's numbing pepper is native. Its fire is not.
3. Hungarian paprika came from the Ottoman Empire, which got it from Portugal, which got it from Brazil
4. Thai food without chilies — the four centuries nobody talks about
5. Vindaloo is Portuguese. So is the chili in it.
6. Why West African cuisine adopted capsicum faster than Europe did
7. Italy has no native chili. Calabria disagrees.
8. The Ethiopian berbere problem — dating the arrival
9. Why did Europe, who carried them everywhere, barely eat them?
10. Birds, mammals, and why chilies invented heat in the first place
11. The one continent chilies never conquered (and why)
12. How long did it actually take? A 100-year map

Hook formula for this series: state the destination cuisine, then remove the pepper from it. "Before 1500, kimchi was white." Two seconds. Then explain.

### Series 2 — Trade Routes

Premise: the 18 timeline events already on your site, one per episode, in order. This is the backbone series — it turns casual viewers into subscribers because it has a next.

Format: 90–120 seconds. Vertical, but shoot these so they also cut together into a long-form YouTube documentary later (see Part 7). Each ends with a card: "Next: 1498, Calicut."

Structure per episode:
* The date and the port, stated flatly, over the map
* What was actually happening there — the human scene
* What moved, and who moved it
* What changed as a result
* Route line drawn on the map, connecting to the previous episode

Use your existing 1492–1600 timeline as the episode list. The 18 events are already sequenced; do not redesign them. The map animation is the through-line that makes this series unmistakably yours — every episode should feature the route line extending further across the same chart.

Serialization device: an on-screen route ledger that fills in as the series progresses. By episode 18 the viewer has watched a blank map become the whole world. That final frame is your channel trailer.

### Series 3 — One Pepper, One Port

Premise: one cultivar, 60 seconds, tied to a place. This is your volume engine and your SEO tie-in — every episode maps 1:1 to a Compendium entry.

Format: 45–60 seconds. Rigid template so you can produce these in batches of 20.

Template:
* Pod on screen, name spoken (0–3s)
* Heat in relative terms, not SHU alone — "nine times a jalapeño" (3–8s)
* Origin and route (8–25s)
* Flavor, not just heat (25–40s)
* One use, one growing note (40–55s)
* "Full entry in the Compendium" (55–60s)

Priority order for the first 40: highest search volume first. Carolina Reaper, ghost pepper / bhut jolokia, habanero, jalapeño, serrano, poblano, scotch bonnet, Thai bird, cayenne, chipotle, Hatch, Aleppo, gochugaru, Kashmiri, guajillo, ancho, pequin, tabasco, datil, fatalii, aji amarillo, rocoto, piri piri, calabrian, shishito, padrón, banana, Anaheim, chile de árbol, Trinidad scorpion, 7 pot, Pepper X, Espelette, urfa biber, Sichuan facing-heaven, byadgi, malagueta, cascabel, mulato, pasilla.

Then work outward into obscurity. The obscure ones get less traffic individually but they are what makes you the reference rather than a listicle.

### Series 4 — Consortium Journeys

Premise: each blend you sell is a place and a century. The video is not an ad. It is a four-minute historical short about a time and place, and the blend is what it left behind.

This is the right instinct and it is your strongest brand differentiator. Nobody in the pepper space is doing narrative product world-building. Treat these as the prestige tier — longer, slower, better-produced, fewer of them.

Format: 3–5 minutes. Horizontal primary (YouTube), cut down to a 60-second vertical trailer.

Cradle of Fire — Mesoamerica, c. 5000 BCE to 1520 CE
* Episode 1: The wild bird pepper and the people who first tamed it
* Episode 2: Chili in Aztec tribute, medicine, and punishment
* Episode 3: The market at Tlatelolco — what a pepper cost in 1519
* Episode 4: What Cortés saw, and what he failed to notice

Other Consortium candidates, each a place-and-period package:
* Levantine Cargo — Ottoman Aleppo, 1550–1700
* The Malabar Consignment — Portuguese Goa and Calicut, 1498–1600
* Heaven-Facing — Sichuan and the Ming-Qing transition
* The Guinea Coast — West Africa and the Atlantic exchange
* Magyar Fire — Hungary under Ottoman occupation, paprika's arrival
* Bonnet & Scorpion — the Caribbean, sugar, and the enslaved kitchens where scotch bonnet cuisine was actually built

That last one carries real historical weight. Handle it seriously or leave it out — a half-treated version would be worse than nothing.

Production note: hold this series until a blend actually exists to sell. The video without the product is a missed conversion; the product without the video is just a spice bag.

## Part 3 — Tool Stack and Learning Path

You said you know nothing. Here is the sequence that gets you from zero to publishing in about two weeks without wasting money.

### Stage 0 — Week 1, spend $0

Goal: publish one video, badly, to learn the pipeline shape.

* Script: Claude Code, from your own site content
* Voice: ElevenLabs free tier (attribution required, non-commercial — this is a throwaway test only)
* Visuals: screen-record your own origins map, plus public-domain historical imagery from the Library of Congress, Rijksmuseum, and Wikimedia Commons. Old maps and engravings are in the public domain and they match your aesthetic better than any AI output will.
* Edit: CapCut, free. Learn it on this video.
* Publish: nowhere. This one is practice.

The single most useful thing here is discovering that editing, not generation, is where the time goes.

### Stage 1 — Weeks 2–6, roughly $30/month

Goal: publish 3x/week, establish the look.

| Need | Tool | Cost | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Script | Claude Code | included | Your site is the source material |
| Voice | ElevenLabs Creator | $22/mo | Commercial rights and Professional Voice Cloning |
| Captions | CapCut auto-captions | free | Adequate at this stage |
| Edit | CapCut | free | Fine until it isn't |
| Stills | Public domain archives | free | Better than AI for your aesthetic |
| Map motion | Screen recording your own site | free | Your unfair advantage |

Deliberately no generative video yet. At this stage you are proving format and hook, and generative video is the most expensive way to look like everyone else.

### Stage 2 — Weeks 7+, roughly $60–120/month

Add generative video only where stills genuinely fail — motion establishing shots, ships under sail, market scenes, harvest. Candidates as of mid-2026: Veo 3.1 (best quality, native audio, expensive), Kling 3.0 (noted as strong for historical storytelling), Seedance (cheap b-roll). Multi-model studios that pool credits across Veo, Kling, and ElevenLabs under one subscription are worth a look purely to avoid managing four billing relationships.

Add at this stage:
* Submagic or CapCut Pro for captions with a locked brand style
* A scheduler that cross-posts to Shorts, Reels, and TikTok in one action
* Suno or similar for a consistent music bed if you want an original theme

### Learning order

Do not try to learn these simultaneously. One per week:

1. CapCut — timeline, cuts, text, captions, export at 1080x1920
2. ElevenLabs — voice selection, pacing, pronunciation overrides (you will fight it over "chinense," "jalapeño," "Tlatelolco")
3. Public domain sourcing — Library of Congress maps, Rijksmuseum, Internet Archive, Wikimedia
4. Prompting a video model — style consistency is the whole skill; write one style block and reuse it verbatim
5. Batch workflow — script 10, voice 10, assemble 10, schedule 10

## Part 4 — Your Own Voice

Yes, and it is the right call for this brand.

ElevenLabs offers two paths:

Instant Voice Cloning — needs 1 to 3 minutes of clean audio, available on the cheapest paid tier. Fast, usable, but drifts on long passages and unusual words. Fine for testing.

Professional Voice Cloning — trains a dedicated model, requires 30 minutes minimum with about 3 hours recommended for production quality, available on the Creator tier at roughly $22/month. This is the one that holds up across a recurring narrated series and captures breathing and emotional range.

Practical recommendation: go straight to Creator and do PVC properly. Record 60 to 90 minutes in one sitting, same room, same mic, same time of day. Consistency of the source recording matters more than the microphone. A $100 dynamic USB mic (Shure MV7, Audio-Technica AT2005) in a closet full of clothes will outperform an expensive mic in a bare room.

What to record: read your own site copy. The heritage section, the trading company philosophy, several cultivar entries, a few timeline events. This gets you a clone trained on the exact vocabulary and cadence you will actually be narrating, including all the pepper names.

Two things to know:
* Commercial rights require a paid plan at generation time and cannot be applied retroactively to free-tier audio.
* Pure AI output has no US copyright protection. You can sell it; you cannot stop someone else from using an identical output. Not a practical concern for narration, but relevant if you ever license the material.

Consider recording the Consortium Journeys narration yourself for real and reserving the clone for the high-volume series. The prestige tier benefits from the imperfections.

## Part 5 — Visual Identity Lock

Pick this once and never deviate. Consistency is the difference between a brand and a content farm.

Proposed lock:

* Palette: aged parchment, iron gall ink black, oxblood red, brass. Your site already runs this. Sample the actual hex values from your CSS and put them in a one-page style sheet.
* Type: whatever your logo uses for display, one clean serif for body. Two faces total, forever.
* Motion vocabulary: slow push on stills. Route lines drawing across the map. Compass rose as transition wipe. No zoom bursts, no shake, no trending transitions.
* Frame furniture: a thin ruled border on every video, like a printed broadside. Costs nothing, instantly identifiable in a feed.
* Corner mark: your compass rose, bottom right, always. Small.
* Caption style: one style, one position, one color. Locked.
* Audio: one music bed family — low strings, distant sea, rigging creak. One narrator. One outro line, spoken identically every time.

Candidate sign-off, spoken over the compass rose: "Recorded in the ledger of the Hot Pepper Trading Company." Or shorter: "So it is entered in the record."

That repeated line is worth more than it looks. It is what people quote back at you in comments, and it is what turns viewers into a group with an in-joke.

## Part 6 — Brand Strengthening

You asked for a memorable niche people want to belong to. That requires three things you do not yet have: a role for the audience, a vocabulary they can use, and objects they can own.

### Give the audience a role

Right now the site talks about the company. It does not offer the visitor a position. Fix this. Options, from most to least formal:

* Subscribers are the Consignment List. Signing up is being entered on the manifest.
* Buyers receive a Bill of Lading with the order — an actual printed card with the blend's route, period, and cultivars.
* Long-term customers accrue a rank. Deckhand, Factor, Supercargo, Master. Purely cosmetic, tracked on the site, costs you nothing and is exactly the kind of thing chilehead communities engage with.

### Fix the founding conceit

"Est. 1493" is charming and slightly risky. Sharpen it into something clearly stylized rather than falsely historical. Suggested framing, stated once on the About page: the Company dates itself to the year the first peppers crossed the Atlantic, because that is when the trade it keeps the record of began. That converts a dubious claim into a stated conceit, which is stronger and more defensible.

### Build the vocabulary

You have started this and should push it further. Consistent, everywhere, no synonyms:

* Cargo, not products
* Consortium, not blend or collection
* Compendium, not database or wiki
* Manifest, not order
* Consignment, not inventory or drop
* Bill of Lading, not receipt
* Trading Post, not shop
* Factor, for a supplier or grower partner
* The Record or the Ledger, for the history section

The rule that makes this work: never break character in the copy, but never let the character obstruct the utility. A user who wants the Scoville number for a habanero must get it in under two seconds regardless of how the page is dressed.

### Merch and giftability

Your visual identity is genuinely giftable, which is rare in this category and is the real commercial asset. Ranked by margin and brand fit:

* The Origins Map, printed. Large format, aged stock. This is your single best product and it has nothing to do with peppers being edible. Price at $45–85.
* The Compendium, printed. A proper book, print-on-demand initially. This is the thing people give their pepper-obsessed brother-in-law.
* Consortium tins with Bill of Lading cards. Physical spice in a tin that looks like cargo. The card is what makes it a gift rather than a groceries item.
* Seed consortiums by route. Envelopes, low cost, no food regulation, sold seasonally in January. Highest-margin, lowest-risk first product you can ship.
* A broadside poster of 25 cultivars by heat — this doubles as your newsletter lead magnet in digital form and a paid print in physical form.
* Enamel compass rose pin, canvas cargo tote, ship's log notebook for growers.

Check Colorado cottage food rules before packaging anything edible. Seeds and paper have no such constraint, which is why they should come first.

### The community layer

The Ship's Log: growers log their season on your site. Variety, sow date, first pod, notes. You get user-generated content, seasonal return visits, and a reason for people to have accounts. This is the single highest-leverage feature you could build after the Compendium and the comparison tool.

## Part 7 — 90-Day Rollout

Weeks 1–2
* Record 90 minutes of clean voice audio; start PVC training
* Build the one-page visual style sheet from your existing site CSS
* Produce and discard one practice video
* Write the full Great Dispersal slate as finished scripts (Claude Code, from your history pages)

Weeks 3–6
* Publish Great Dispersal, 3 per week, all platforms
* Lock caption style, outro line, corner mark
* Add newsletter capture to every video description and a dedicated landing page
* Ship the printable broadside as the newsletter lead magnet

Weeks 5–10
* Add Trade Routes, 1 per week, in strict chronological order
* Build the map animation workflow once, reuse for all 18
* Begin the comparison tool on the site (Bill of Lading), since Great Dispersal traffic will be arriving

Weeks 9–13
* Add One Pepper One Port, batched 20 at a time, 2 per week published
* Each video description links its Compendium entry
* Push Compendium completion for the top 40 cultivars specifically

Week 12+
* Cut the 18 Trade Routes episodes into a single 30-minute YouTube documentary. This is the asset that gets you taken seriously and it costs almost nothing since the material exists.
* Launch seed consortiums to coincide with January seed-starting season
* Produce Cradle of Fire episode 1 as the Consortium Journeys pilot

### What to measure

Ignore views. Track:
* Retention at 3 seconds (hook working or not)
* Click-through to the site per 1,000 views
* Newsletter signups per video
* Which of the four series converts best, then reallocate

## Honest risks

* Volume burnout is the standard failure mode. 190 episodes of One Pepper One Port is only achievable with a rigid template and batch production. If you find yourself hand-crafting each one, cut the target to 40 and stop.
* Historical accuracy will be tested in the comments, and this audience includes serious growers and food historians. One wrong date becomes the video's legacy. Source the timeline claims properly and be willing to pin a correction.
* AI-generated video is increasingly labeled and increasingly disliked. Your defense is that the majority of your visuals are public domain archival material and your own map — genuinely different from a synthetic pipeline. Lean into that publicly rather than hiding it.
* The videos will produce traffic before the site can convert it. Do not launch the channel before the Compendium can answer the questions the videos raise.
